# Review of the A2 finding, relayed by Daniel on 2026-09-11 (author not named in the relay)

Verbatim as received. The Chief's evaluation and the experiments it triggered are in the README, §4, and in the bridge order.

---

**My leading diagnosis is that two problems may coexist: the dictionary misses some useful information, and our current metric may overstate how much that matters for a decision.** We should separate those before treating larger dictionaries as the solution.

I checked the implementation, existing result files and the relevant papers. Three findings change the investigation:

- **The current gate measures unnormalised lens scores across the whole vocabulary.** It does not directly measure changes in the model's probabilities or actions.
- **The apparent failure is uneven.** In the earlier sparse-dictionary run at 4B layer 24, all 300 action positions failed the vocabulary-wide gate, but the median relative error for the recorded target token's score was only **14.6%**. That target comes from the expert corpus; it is not necessarily what the model would freely choose. Competing-token scores still need checking.
- **"In domain" was too broad a description.** The control uses the lens's prose-fitting domain. Google's instruction-tuned dictionaries were adapted on Gemma chat rollouts. Google also fine-tuned selected dictionaries against downstream behaviour; the inspected configurations do not identify whether ours received that treatment. We cannot yet assert that their training objective was solely reconstruction error. [Google's technical paper](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/DeepMind.com/Blog/gemma-scope-2-helping-the-ai-safety-community-deepen-understanding-of-complex-language-model-behavior/Gemma_Scope_2_Technical_Paper.pdf)

Here are the experiments I would propose, in order.

**1. Test whether the metric is counting errors that barely affect token choice.**

A common shift applied to every final logit changes no token probabilities. Errors among thousands of extremely unlikely tokens can also contribute substantially to a vocabulary-wide norm while scarcely affecting the next decision.

**Simple experiment:** On the same saved activations, compare the original and reconstructed readouts using:

- The existing vocabulary-wide error.
- Error after subtracting each score vector's vocabulary mean.
- Changes in the score gaps between plausible competing tokens.
- The winning token and its margin over the runner-up.

Record absolute error magnitudes and denominators separately, too: a large ratio can arise because its reference is small.

**Supports this explanation:** The global error remains large while relevant score gaps and rankings barely move.

**Weakens it:** The error remains large after centring and regularly reverses substantial margins.

**Resolution:** Add a separately specified, decision-focused fidelity measure. Preserve the existing gate and its historical verdicts; do not retrospectively relax it until the results pass.

---

**2. Test whether the dictionary's errors are unusually damaging—or whether almost any error would be amplified.**

An "11× amplification" compares the error's amplification with that of the original activation. **It does not establish that the error is worse than a random error of the same size.** The original activation could contain large components to which the readout is relatively insensitive.

**Simple experiment:** For each saved activation, compare its actual reconstruction error with random errors matched for magnitude. Include a stronger control that also matches the angle and reconstructed activation norm. Measure both through the same lens.

**Supports unusually harmful error directions:** Actual errors consistently produce larger changes than these controls, including in the upper tail.

**Weakens it:** Actual errors fall within the controls' distribution.

**Resolution:** If actual errors are exceptional, focus on which directions the dictionary systematically misses. If ordinary perturbations behave similarly, the headline amplification mostly describes the readout's sensitivity.

This follows an established experiment: Gurnee found SAE substitutions could damage GPT-2 predictions more than matched random perturbations. That is a precedent to test here, not an established explanation of our Gemma results. [Original experiments](https://www.alignmentforum.org/posts/rZPiuFxESMxCDHe4B/sae-reconstruction-errors-are-empirically-pathological)

---

**3. Test the model directly, to distinguish dictionary failure from lens failure.**

This is the decisive experiment.

**Simple experiment:** At a fixed decision position, run the remaining model with:

1. The original activation.
2. The dictionary reconstruction.
3. Matched random substitutions.
4. A same-state replacement control.

Measure the change in the actual next-token distribution, winning-token margin, and—on a small subset—the completed tool call.

Compare those changes with the lens's predictions. At a few positions, use an exact local derivative along the reconstruction-error direction, rather than fitting another complete lens.

**Possible outcomes:**

| Observation | Interpretation |
|---|---|
| Lens predicts large disruption; model changes little | Lens or readout metric overstates functional damage. |
| Local derivative agrees; averaged lens disagrees | Averaging or the lens's domain is the likely limitation. |
| Actual reconstruction damages predictions more than matched controls | Genuine functional reconstruction failure. |
| Small substitutions agree with the derivative; full reconstruction does not | Nonlinearity matters at the reconstruction's actual size. |

The current linear bridge deliberately omits the input-dependent RMS-normalisation factor. That preserves its algebraic decomposition, but makes direct model confirmation particularly important.

---

**4. Test whether the dictionary has useful features but activates them incorrectly.**

There are two distinct limitations: **which feature directions exist**, and **how the encoder chooses their coefficients**.

**Simple experiment:** Freeze the dictionary's decoder. For each activation:

- Refit nonnegative coefficients using only its currently active features.
- Then allow a different selection of features, keeping the same maximum number active.

Use the activation itself as the reconstruction target; no future-token labels are needed.

**Interpretation:**

- Improvement with the same active features implicates coefficient estimation.
- Improvement only after changing the active set implicates feature selection.
- Little improvement in either arm points toward a limitation of the available sparse representation.

**Resolution:** Correct the encoder or coefficient estimation if that suffices. A larger dictionary is warranted only if the existing one cannot produce a sufficiently faithful sparse reconstruction.

An unrestricted dense fit would be an inadequate control: an overcomplete dictionary can reconstruct well by using many features while losing the sparse explanation we wanted.

---

**5. Read the width experiment as a paired comparison, not a single leaderboard.**

The larger dictionaries already running are useful. Compare them on **identical positions**, reporting achieved active-feature counts, raw error, decision-focused error and actual behavioural preservation.

The 65k files come from a different dictionary suite than the all-layer 16k files. Add a 16k dictionary from that same suite where available, so suite differences are not silently attributed to width.

**Supports a capacity explanation:** Increasing width improves functional preservation at comparable sparsity across held-out episodes.

**Weakens it:** Raw reconstruction improves while behavioural preservation stalls.

Density has already helped some layer-24 measurements. It should remain a factor under investigation.

---

**6. Check domain and precision with a small matched control.**

Compare short raw prose, short chat-formatted material and agent histories. Separately compare native bf16 and whole-model float32 on identical tokens.

Before that, compare our encoder against the reference implementation on a few identical saved activations. Google folds both normalisation and the pre-encoder bias into the released parameters; our formula follows that convention. Adding another normalisation would be an incorrect repair. [Google's inference conventions](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/DeepMind.com/Blog/gemma-scope-2-helping-the-ai-safety-community-deepen-understanding-of-complex-language-model-behavior/Gemma_Scope_2_Technical_Paper.pdf)

A strong dependence on formatting or precision would support domain adaptation or precision-matched calibration. Persistence across those controls would weaken that explanation.

**What I would do first**

Start with the saved-array tests in **1, 2 and 4**, then one protected model session for **3**, initially on 32 preselected episodes at layers 18 and 24. Treat that as a diagnostic screen. Report paired episode results, tails and failures—not only medians.

If functional damage remains after these checks, the strongest established remedy is a small dictionary fine-tune combining reconstruction with downstream probability preservation. Such fine-tuning has improved fidelity in other models, but interpretability improvements are not automatic; downstream internal states should also be checked. [Braun et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.12241v2), [Karvonen](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.17272v2)

**The next decisive finding should be whether substituting the reconstruction changes the model's decision, and whether the lens predicts that change correctly.** No model runs or source changes were made in this investigation.
